Search Intelligence Capstone · Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which pages should you refresh first? A validated answer from real search data.

Question. Given a large portfolio of content pages, which ones are worth reviewing for a refresh first — and how much better than guesswork can a data-driven queue actually get?

Data. A real, pseudonymized cohort from FlyRank's search-and-analytics warehouse: 145,279 client-content pairs across 42 active clients, observed in February 2026 and checked against their own outcome the following March.

Method. A fully transparent baseline rule, then two classifiers validated under both a naive random split and a client-held-out split — built specifically to catch whether a model generalizes or just memorizes.

Result. The naive split flattered a Random Forest into near-perfect (98–100%) apparent precision; forcing it to generalize to unseen clients dropped that to an honest 70% (3.5× over a 20% baseline). A simpler logistic regression showed the opposite pattern, better under the honest split (78–85%) — reported as promising, not proven, on a small held-out sample.

Use. The result ships as a working, human-reviewed priority queue with a volume floor, reason codes, and a monitoring plan — built to support a content team's decisions, not replace them.

01

Introduction

A content team managing hundreds of thousands of pages across dozens of clients can't manually review everything every month. They need an ordered queue: what to look at first, and why.

Here's the problem with a simple "is this page declining?" filter: a page that dropped from 50,000 monthly impressions to 40,000 gets the same label as a page that dropped from 20 impressions to 15. Both are technically "declining," but no reviewer would treat them the same way. The real decision isn't binary — it's an ordering. That makes this a ranking / scoring problem, not a classification problem, and it changes which tools and which metrics actually apply.

This paper walks the full arc: a fully transparent rule anyone could audit, a validated model that had to earn the right to be trusted more than that rule, the honest gaps found along the way, and a working playbook a person can actually run. The mistakes caught and fixed in the process are reported alongside the final numbers — they're part of the evidence, not an embarrassment to edit out.

02

Data

Source: the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, a gated dataset released on Hugging Face (credit and link in Acknowledgments). All identifiers in this dataset — and everywhere in this paper — are pseudonymous hashes; no client name, domain, or real query appears anywhere.

Tables used: the daily performance fact table (partitioned by month), joined to client and content dimension tables for tracking-history and content-age metadata.

Time window: February 2026 as the feature month, March 2026 as a genuinely forward-looking outcome month — not a same-window relabeling of the same data.

Scale: 7.35 million daily (date × client × content) rows in February alone, across 54 clients with any tracked activity that month — rolling up to 145,279 distinct (client, content) pairs with real traffic in both February and March, across the 42 of those clients with at least one such pair.

What was excluded, and why

  • The sealed final month in the release and its reduced-sample companion table are never used for feature or label development — they're the natural "answer key" for any past-to-future label and are reserved as a held-out check only.
  • Rows before a client's own tracking start date are excluded via explicit availability flags, never treated as real zero activity.
  • No raw queries, URLs, or client-identifying information of any kind are used or shown — only the warehouse's own pseudonymous identifiers.
Data quality, stated plainly

GA4 (engagement) tracking is present for only about 2% of February's daily rows, versus roughly 35.6% for search-console data — even within one ordinary month, not just at each client's tracking start. This single fact explains why close to half of all (client, content) pairs carry no usable GA4-derived signal at all, and why the modeling work below excludes them from scoring rather than guessing.

03

Methodology

Task framing

This is a ranking/scoring task. The label used — "did this page's impressions fall by 20% or more from February to March" — is a defined proxy, not an observed ground truth. No one has ever recorded a judgment of "this page needed a refresh" for these pages; the proxy stands in for it, and is named as a proxy throughout.

Features

Everything used is knowable at the end of February, before March exists: total search impressions and clicks, average search position, GA4 sessions, AI-referral sessions, content age in days, and a within-February "already fading" flag (comparing the first half of the month against the second) — built deliberately so no feature can ever see March's outcome.

Baseline

An intentionally simple, fully auditable rule: flag a page if it falls in an empirically checked elevated-risk age band (90–365 days — a naive "older is always worse" assumption was tested against real data and rejected) and carries enough traffic to matter (≥500 monthly impressions, itself checked against real bucket data rather than assumed), then rank by raw impression volume as a stand-in for the size of the opportunity.

Validation design — the central decision in this project

A naive random row-level split can put 80% of a given client's pages into training and test on the remaining 20% of the same client — rewarding a model for memorizing that client's own baseline traffic levels, not for learning something that generalizes to a client it has never seen. Every model result below is reported under both a naive random split and a client-held-out split, so the gap between them is visible rather than hidden.

Leakage checks — two, not one

A static check confirms no feature touches the March outcome or any product-decision field. A dynamic check goes further: it deliberately injects the literal March outcome column as if it were a feature, confirms precision jumps to a suspicious 100%, then confirms it collapses back down once removed — proving the test harness would actually catch a real leak, not just assert one doesn't exist.

Auditing someone else's claims, the same way

The same scrutiny was turned on FlyRank's own public research paper, which inspired this format. Two open, respectfully-framed methodology questions: does a headline "52× traffic lift from refreshing" figure separate the effect of refreshing from the effect of choosing which pages to refresh (selection vs. causation)? And do three reported model-accuracy figures ever state the base rate needed to distinguish real skill from a majority-class guess? Neither is an accusation — it's the same standard applied to this paper's own numbers below.

04

Results

The baseline rule scores Precision@20 of 30.0% against a 26.1% base rate — a real but thin 1.15× lift. That's the bar the model has to clear, and it's a low one on purpose: a baseline should be beatable.

Precision@20 by model and split. The gap between random and grouped splits — not either single number — is the finding: Random Forest's apparent near-perfect score was mostly client memorization; logistic regression showed the opposite pattern on a small held-out sample.

The honest finding is the split, not a single number

Random Forest's precision collapsed from a suspicious 98–100% under the naive split to a real, trustworthy 70% once client identity could no longer be memorized — a textbook case of validation leakage through group structure, caught and corrected by design, not by accident.

A result that deserves scrutiny, not a victory lap

Logistic regression went the other way — higher under the grouped split (85%) than the random split (40%). This is measured on only 5 held-out clients, a thin, noisy sample where one unusual client can swing the whole number, and that slice happens to carry a higher base rate (40% vs. 30.5%), which mechanically inflates raw precision somewhat. The direction still favors the grouped split after correcting for that with lift (2.1× vs. 1.3×), but this paper treats 85% as promising, not proven without repeating the split across different held-out client groups.

Where the model is actually wrong

Calibration is well-behaved at the extremes: excellent at very low traffic (41.4% actual vs. 41.1% predicted decline rate for pages under 100 impressions — exactly the segment the baseline rule discards as noise) and reasonable at moderate-to-high traffic (14.8% vs. 14.7% for 2,000–10,000 impressions). It under-predicts risk by roughly 10 points in the 100–2,000 impression middle range.

Its clearest false positives are pages with single-digit monthly impressions (3, 7, and 2 impressions) — small-sample noise, not real signal. Its clearest false negatives are moderate-traffic pages (530, 184, and 1,155 impressions) with zero warning sign anywhere in February itself — a real structural limit: no February-only feature set can foresee a decline that has no February-internal precursor.

Leakage, confirmed both ways

Deliberately injecting the literal March outcome as a feature drove precision@20 to 100.0%; removing it dropped it back to the 85.0% honest number above — a 15-point collapse confirming the test harness is sensitive to leakage, not blind to it.

05

Limitations & honest framing

  • This is cross-sectional, single-cohort data — one February→March transition. It does not establish that refreshing a flagged page causes recovery; no experiment was run, and no claim here says otherwise.
  • The 85% logistic-regression figure rests on 5 held-out clients. Treat 70% (Random Forest's grouped-split number) as the floor this recommendation should clear; treat 85% as an unconfirmed upside.
  • Roughly half the panel (49.9%) has no usable GA4 signal and is excluded from scoring entirely — never silently guessed at as "low priority."
  • The deployed model cannot foresee a decline with no within-February warning sign — a genuinely sudden, unprecedented drop will be missed regardless of tuning.
  • Every identifier used is a pseudonymous hash from FlyRank's own release; no client, domain, or query is named anywhere in this paper or its underlying repository.
06

Ranked recommendations — the playbook

The validated model above ships as a working, four-tier priority queue. A volume floor of 500 monthly impressions gates which pages can ever enter an actionable tier, however high their raw score — a fix added after catching a real failure mode mid-build (see callout).

4,988 review
17,459 monitor
47,908 insufficient_volume (excluded)
A mistake, left in the record on purpose

An earlier version of this pipeline ranked purely by raw model probability. Its top 20 recommendations were entirely pages with 1–8 monthly impressions — a "decline" from 1 impression to 0 is a 100% drop that means nothing. Adding the volume floor fixed this: every page now in the top tier carries real within-February decline evidence, not just size or age, and 0 of the 2,494 refresh_priority pages lack that evidence.

Before acting, a person should check

  • Whether the decline looks seasonal rather than structural.
  • Whether a tracking or tagging change explains the drop rather than real audience loss.
  • Whether the page is still strategically relevant at all — the model has no concept of business priority, only traffic pattern.

What should never be automated

  • No auto-publishing or auto-editing content from a score alone.
  • No treating monitor as "ignore permanently" — it means recheck next cycle.
  • No using this score as sole justification for removing or de-indexing a page.
  • No applying this specific model to a brand-new client with no February–March history of its own.

Retrain triggers

  • Real-world precision@20 falling below the 70% floor for two consecutive review cycles.
  • The observed base decline rate drifting meaningfully outside the 30–40% range seen here.
  • The share of unscorable (missing-GA4) rows growing well beyond the current ~50%.
  • At minimum, once per quarter regardless — this model has only ever seen one month's transition.
